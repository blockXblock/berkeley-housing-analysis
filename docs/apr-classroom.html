For the Possibility Lab and the California Department of Housing and Community Development · draft, 2026-10-28

Students and planners, one APR

A high-school class and its city's planning staff build the city's Annual Progress Report together, from the city's own records, and check it against what the city files with the state. Then they keep it current all year.

The proposal

Every California city reports its housing production to HCD each year in the Annual Progress Report (APR). Planning staff assemble it under deadline, from permit systems that were not designed to answer HCD's questions. We propose that local high-school students do that assembly as a course, alongside the planners whose job it is: students rebuild the city's housing record from the primary sources, produce their own APR tables, and compare them with the city's filing, row by row. The planners get a second, independent build of their own report; the students learn data science on a question their own city has to answer by law.

The course exists. In Berkeley, the full build has been done from public-records productions and the County assessor, and it is written up as 18 free notebooks that run in a browser. It has now been tested in a second city: San Francisco, from public data alone, came within about 10 percent of the city's own filing. What has not been done yet is the classroom: it has not been taught to high-school students. We propose a pilot.

Tested in a second city: San Francisco

In October 2026 we ran the course's method on San Francisco, a city about seven times Berkeley's population, using only data the city already publishes. No public-records request was needed.

  • The inputs: the Department of Building Inspection's permit table on DataSF (219,481 permits issued or completed 2018–2025), DBI's record of certificates of occupancy (2,559 certificates), and, as the answer key only, San Francisco's APR Table A2 from HCD's portal (5,899 rows).
  • The result: 26,658 completed units for 2018–2025, against 29,685 in the city's filings, a gap of 10 percent. 2,017 buildings agree exactly, on both the number of units and the year they were completed.
  • The gap has named causes, the kind a class would hand to planning staff. San Francisco's filing counts about 1,200 units that have no city building permit: UC Law SF's student housing at 198 McAllister (657 units) and Mission Rock, on Port land, where the Port issues its own permits. Several occupied towers were never closed out in DBI's permit record. And about 1,000 units are our own errors: hotel rooms, and alteration permits that restate a whole building's units, which our first pass counted as new homes.

What HCD gains

  • An independent check on each filing. The students' build never uses the city's APR as an input; HCD's statewide open data is the answer key they grade against. Agreement becomes evidence, and a difference becomes a named, sourced finding. In Berkeley, that comparison has turned up differences in both directions, the city's and our own (the Housing Audit).
  • A method that travels. HCD's portal carries every city's APR, so the answer key exists for every jurisdiction. What changes from city to city is the permit data, which every city holds. San Francisco, the first test outside Berkeley, came within 10 percent from public data alone.
  • Earlier, steadier reporting. A record rebuilt from the permit stream can be kept current month by month, not assembled once a year (see the real-time APR).
  • Local people who understand the numbers. Students and their teachers learn what the APR measures and why the counts are hard, which is the public understanding housing policy depends on.

What students produce

  • Their own APR Table A2: the city's completions, built from the city's permits, written as a spreadsheet in HCD's layout.
  • A scored comparison with the city's filed APR: every unit of difference accounted for and sorted into named causes.
  • A ledger of decisions: for each difference, what the student found, the evidence, and what should change, in the city's record or their own.

These are the artifacts a planner can use: a list of specific permits to look at, each with its evidence.

Who does what

  • Students work through the notebooks, build the tables, and investigate the differences.
  • The teacher runs the course; no prior coding is required of students, and the first nine notebooks build the vocabulary from zero.
  • Planning staff provide the permit export (or answer the public-records request), meet the class a few times in the term, and review the students' list of differences.
  • The Possibility Lab would design and evaluate the pilot: what students learn, what planners use, and what it costs a city.

Time, prerequisites and standards

  • Prerequisites: a browser and a Google account (a school Google Workspace account works) for free Colab notebooks. Nothing to install.
  • Time: 18 notebooks, the first nine short. We expect about a semester at one or two notebooks a week; the pilot should measure it.
  • Standards: the course touches data science and statistics in the California Mathematics Framework, and civics through state and local government. Mapping it to specific standards is work to do with the pilot's teachers.

How another city starts

San Francisco showed the steps, and where the work is.

  1. Get the permit data. Some cities publish it: San Francisco's permit table and its certificates of occupancy are both on DataSF. Others export it from their permit portal, or produce it under a public-records request for the annual permit report, as Berkeley did.
  2. Map that city's columns onto the course's. This is the real work. San Francisco records existing and proposed units rather than units added, so "new homes" has to be worked out, and its permit table does not mark every finished building complete, so the certificates of occupancy are a second source. The counting rules themselves (what is a new home, when is it complete, which cycle it counts toward) carried over unchanged.
  3. Point the answer key at the city. The city's APR comes from HCD's portal by jurisdiction name. In San Francisco it records DBI's permit number on most rows, which makes the comparison easier than in Berkeley.

Today the notebooks carry Berkeley's settings in each notebook. Gathering a city's settings into one place, so a class changes them once, is work we will finish before a pilot.

The real-time APR

The APR is an annual snapshot of a stream: permits are filed, issued and finaled every week. In Berkeley we already harvest the city's permit portal by date range, and the last notebook teaches students to re-check the state's copy of the filing on a schedule and flag what changed. Put together, a city's APR tables can be rebuilt whenever new permits arrive, so the annual filing becomes a check of a record that was kept all year. That has not yet been run as a standing service; it is the natural second phase of a pilot.

Privacy

The course uses public records only: building permits, planning records, and County parcel data. Students do not submit personal information, and the notebooks collect none.

Questions we expect

Is this about catching cities out?
No. A difference between two builds is a question, not an accusation, and in Berkeley many of the differences have been our own errors. The city's planners are partners in the course, and they see every finding first.
Do planning staff have time?
The course is designed to take work off them: the students do the assembly and hand back a short list of specific permits to review. The staff time is the data export and a few class visits.
What if a student gets a number wrong?
That is what the comparison is for. Every number is checked against a source the student did not build, and every difference has to be explained with evidence before it counts as a finding.
What does it cost?
The notebooks and their hosting are free to use. The costs are teacher time, planner time, and the pilot's evaluation.
Does it work outside Berkeley?
Yes. San Francisco, from public data alone, came within 10 percent of its own filings, with 2,017 buildings agreeing exactly (above). Each new city needs its permit columns mapped, and sometimes a second source for completions.
Who maintains it?
The notebooks and data are public on GitHub, and every rebuild re-runs its own checks: the San Francisco test caught a column HCD had renamed, and it was fixed the same day. A pilot would settle who supports teachers during the term.
