What the numbers say

Nine findings

Nine findings from the data in this report. Each sentence can be checked against the section and the data file linked beneath it. The City's Vision 2050 work, which looks ahead to the same streets, pipes and energy systems, is cited where it addressed the same question.

A university city, and a commuter city

127,654 people live in Berkeley; 21,609 of them, one in six, live in dorms, co-ops and other group housing. Each workday 63,519 people commute in and 35,994 residents commute out, so Berkeley gains about 27,500 workers by day, before counting 46,151 UC Berkeley students. The largest employer sector is education: 21,033 of the city's 73,292 jobs.

People · Daily tide · data: lodes_2023_inflow_by_home_place.csv

Most households rent, and most renters are stretched

54.1% of households rent. 56.8% of renter households pay 30% or more of their income in rent, and 8,859, about one in three, pay half or more. Incomes split at both ends: 14,718 households earn under $50,000 and 14,921 earn $200,000 or more.

Renters and owners · Income · maps: berkeley_blockgroups.geojson

How people move has already changed

31.5% of employed residents work from home, nearly as many as drive alone to work (32.4%). 20.3% of households have no car. Of 62,961 registered vehicles, 9,492 (15%) are electric or plug-in hybrids. In 2020 the City's Vision 2050 framework wrote that it was “unknown what alternative forms of transportation might emerge and how personal transportation behaviors might change”; the working-from-home figure answers part of that. Autonomous vehicles and home deliveries, which the 2017 Berkeley Drawdown list flagged, are not yet counted by any public source.

185 of 219 street miles still carry overhead wires

Only 16% of Berkeley's street miles have their wires underground: half of the arterials, a third of the collectors, and 7% of residential streets. The 147 miles of overhead residential streets are priced in none of the City cost studies we found. The poles that carry the wires are not public; from the 4,499 wood poles the City has hung streetlights on, we estimate about 7,400–8,300 street poles. Vision 2050 pictured a city whose evacuation routes had been undergrounded; a 2019 City study priced 16.9 miles of those routes at $93.3 million.

Undergrounding · Under the streets · data: undergrounding_segments_harris_2016.csv, wood_pole_lines_traced.geojson

The City's undergrounding cost is $5.4 million short

The 2016 cost table behind every City undergrounding estimate prices Ashby Ave's five residential-zone segments at $5,387,150 but leaves them out of its totals. Summed segment by segment, burying the wires on the remaining arterial and collector streets costs $140,174,450, not the $134.8 million quoted since 2016, in 2016 dollars and before engineering, financing and street lighting.

Undergrounding · data: undergrounding_segments_harris_2016.csv

The pipes are old; the money for them shrank

Half of the pipe segments in Berkeley's 269 miles of water main were laid before 1950, and 120 miles before 1940. The sewers have been rebuilt since 1986, most of them relined in plastic. Vision 2050 documented the City's annual infrastructure funding falling from $53 million to $40 million between FY2012 and FY2019, while unfunded needs grew.

Under the streets · Vision 2050 framework (2020) · maps: infrastructure_by_blockgroup.csv

Leaving the hills in a fire takes hours

The City's 2025 evacuation study finds that clearing 90% of vehicles from hill Fire Zones 2 and 3 takes 1:45 to 2:20, and the last vehicles 3:25 to 3:40. One hour in, most roads in the zones are over capacity. 1,547 residential parcels have a single way out. The hill zones hold 8,575 parcels and $8.7 billion of taxable value.

Fire evacuation · data: evacuation_clearance_times_kld_2025.csv

The University and the Lab sit outside the City's numbers, and the campus's reported energy use looks tenfold low

UC Berkeley and Berkeley Lab emitted 195,885 tonnes of CO₂e in 2024, equal to 45% of the City's community inventory, which leaves both out. Most of the campus's share comes from its gas-fired power plant, whose retirement date has moved from 2028 to 2030 to 2033. In its 2025 national sustainability report (STARS), the campus gives its 2023 energy use as 17 kBtu per square foot; the same report's emissions imply about 168, matching the UC system's own figure. The energy entry appears to leave out the power plant's gas.

The University and the Lab · data: stars_2025_ucb_operations.csv, campus_lab_emissions_energy_2009_2024.csv

A $30 billion tax base, with the University outside it

Berkeley's 29,134 parcels carry $30.5 billion of net taxable value, about $376 million a year in base property tax for all agencies. 1,473 parcels pay none: the University, the City, schools, churches and other exempt owners.

How many streets jam?

The study does not count them; it maps every congested road and names them. "One hour into the evacuation, the majority of the roads within the Evacuation Zones are operating at LOS F [demand over capacity] conditions including Grizzly Peak Blvd, Euclid Ave, Spruce St, Marin Ave, La Loma Ave, Gayley Rd, and Arlington Ave." Hill streets carry at most 900 cars an hour (flatland arterials about 1,700), and "severe congestion is expected to occur in all evacuation cases studied." The choke points are intersections, the Marin Avenue five-way roundabout among them, so red-curbing hill streets is "unlikely to substantially improve evacuation flow." Hills residents leaving early, combined with a faster departure, cut the time to clear 90% of vehicles by up to 68% (the last vehicles, by up to 14%). The Fire Department inspects about 8,600 hill parcels a year.

The campus's national sustainability report leaves out its power plant. In its 2025 STARS report the campus gives its 2023 energy use as 78,414 MWh, including 30,014 MWh of natural gas, while describing the gas-fired cogeneration plant as "the primary source for electricity and heating for campus buildings." The same report's emissions credit gives 134,957 tonnes of CO₂e from burning fuel on site in 2023, which at the campus's own factor (53.02 kg of CO₂ per MMBtu) means about 746,000 MWh of gas (the CO₂ factor alone slightly overstates it), some 25 times the energy credit's figure. With that gas, the campus used roughly 794 thousand MWh, about 168 kBtu per square foot, which matches the 163 the UC system report gives for 2023. The STARS energy figure (17 kBtu per square foot) understates the campus's energy use about tenfold. For scale, the campus's gas, about 25 million therms, is six times the 4.2 million therms reported by all of the City's large buildings in 2024. Data: stars_2025_ucb_operations.csv.

Its gas-fired cogeneration plant produced 114,262 of the campus's 160,270 tonnes of CO₂e in 2024; the Berkeley Clean Energy Campus project replaces it with an electric heating and cooling plant serving about 32 buildings in its first phase. The Regents approved a Phase 1 budget of $476.6 million in May 2024 ($249 million of it from the State, through debt service, and the rest from renewable-energy tax credits and campus funds). That budget item included "geothermal heat exchange wells under the plant," the grid of boreholes Professor Kenichi Soga's research tested; the scope the Regents approved in July 2025, after the campus "refined the design and scope… to align with the approved project budget," mentions a 2-million-gallon chilled-water tank but no geothermal wells. The plant goes under North Field, the recreation field north of Hearst Gym, with a grass field on its roof. The retirement date has slid: 2028 in the campus's 2023 fact sheet, "by 2030" in the Regents' 2025 approval (utility relocation from summer 2026, plant construction spring 2027 to summer 2029, building conversions to summer 2030), and 2033 in a May 2024 campus report a STARS reviewer cited. Until then the gas plant runs. The two institutions' 195,885 tonnes equal 45% of the City's own community inventory (432,806 tonnes, 2023), which does not include them; the inventories are compiled differently. (The campus's own inventory page gives 160,270 tonnes for 2024, counting Scope 1 before its 2,090 tonnes of compliance offsets.)
