What we found

The findings, each linked to its section, data and sources. Where the City's Vision 2050 work addressed the same question, it is cited.

A university city, and a commuter city

127,654 people live in Berkeley; 21,609 of them, one in six, live in dorms, co-ops and other group housing. Each workday about 63,500 people commute in and about 36,000 residents commute out, so Berkeley gains about 27,500 workers by day, before counting 46,151 UC Berkeley students. The largest employer sector is education: about 21,000 of about 73,300 jobs in the city.

Room for many more: plans point to nearly 30,000 new residents

Berkeley's population has held near 128,000 since 2020. The State requires the City to plan for 8,934 new homes by 2031; BerkeleyBuild's pipeline counts 11,222 homes approved or proposed but not yet built; and UC Berkeley is building or planning three dormitories with about 4,700 beds, on top of the about 9,800 student beds in Berkeley today. Built out at today's 2.13 people per home, the pipeline alone would house about 23,900 more people, and with the new dormitories the city would approach 156,000. Every new resident adds to the daily load on the same water mains, sewers, power lines and evacuation routes this report describes.

Most households rent, and most renters are stretched

54% of households rent. 57% of renter households pay 30% or more of their income in rent, and about one in three pay half or more. Incomes split at both ends: about 14,700 households earn under $50,000 and about 14,900 earn $200,000 or more.

How people move has already changed

About a third of employed residents work from home, as many as drive alone to work (about 32% each). One household in five has no car. Of 62,961 registered vehicles, 9,492 (15%) are electric or plug-in hybrids, up from 3,985 five years earlier. Shared e-bike trips are growing fast: Bay Wheels rides from Berkeley stations rose 53% in 2025. In 2020 the City's Vision 2050 framework wrote that it was “unknown what alternative forms of transportation might emerge and how personal transportation behaviors might change”; the working-from-home figure answers part of that. Autonomous vehicles and home deliveries, which the 2017 Berkeley Drawdown list flagged, are not yet counted by any public source.

13,004 licensed businesses, most of them small

The City licenses 13,004 businesses, 8,203 of them at a Berkeley address; the rest are mostly contractors and firms based elsewhere that work here. Most are small: 5,034 are sole proprietorships. The most common local businesses are therapists' and counselors' offices, restaurants, beauty salons, architects and lawyers, clustered downtown, in South and Southwest Berkeley and along College Avenue in the Elmwood. They paid $31.6 million in business license tax in FY2025. Berkeley's about 73,300 jobs are dominated by education, about 21,000, followed by professional and technical services and health care.

A small city of ideas, parks and talent

UC Berkeley alumni and faculty hold about 60 Nobel Prizes; five faculty members have won since 2020. The University ranks No. 7 in the world and first among public universities, with physics, chemistry and statistics among the world's top three, and Cal athletes have won 246 Olympic medals, 126 of them gold: more golds than all but 13 nations in the history of the Summer Games. Around it cluster institutes in mathematics, theoretical computer science and artificial intelligence, a new college of computing and data science, and new buildings for climate and genomics startups. The City pairs this with public limits on surveillance technology and principles for its own use of AI. Berkeley has more than fifty parks and a 100-acre waterfront with the Bay's largest public marina, though its park acreage per resident falls short of the State standard.

Ideas, parks and talent

185 of 219 street miles still carry overhead wires

Only 34.6 miles (16%) have their wires underground: half of the arterials, a third of the collectors and 7% of residential streets. Overhead lines are a hazard in each disaster Berkeley plans for. In a fire, burning poles topple across the streets fire engines and evacuees need, and drop live high-voltage lines on homes and people; in the 1991 Oakland fire, falling lines blocked streets, started new fires and cut power to the pumps that supplied the hydrants. In an earthquake or windstorm, swaying poles snap their wires. About 7,400–8,300 poles1 line Berkeley's streets, carrying roughly 7,000–8,000 oil-filled transformers for homes2, and perhaps 10,000 counting businesses, the campus and the Lab. Burying all 185 miles would cost roughly $0.6 to $1.1 billion3; the City's capital plan budgets nothing for it. Even the City's own estimate for the main streets is low: its 2016 cost table prices Ashby Ave's five residential-zone segments at $5,387,150 but leaves them out of its totals, so the arterials and collectors cost $140.2 million in 2016 dollars, not the $134.8 million quoted ever since.

Water and sewer pipes are old, and the money for them shrank

Half of the pipe segments in Berkeley's 269 miles of water main were laid before 1950, and 120 miles before 1940. The City has relined about 180 of its 261 miles of sewer main since 1986, work worth about $450 million at its own current unit costs, and budgets about $24 million a year more. The weak link is the roughly 31,600 house laterals, most as old as the houses they serve; nearly half of Berkeley's homes were built before 1940. Rain leaking through cracked laterals, joints and mains can raise sewage flows more than tenfold in storms, which is why a federal consent decree requires each lateral to be tested, and repaired if it leaks, when a property is sold or substantially remodeled. Vision 2050 documented the City's annual infrastructure funding falling from $53 million to $40 million between FY2012 and FY2019, while unfunded needs grew.

Leaving the hills in a fire takes hours

The City's 2025 evacuation study finds that clearing 90% of vehicles from hill Fire Zones 2 and 3 takes 1:45 to 2:20, and the last vehicles 3:25 to 3:40. One hour in, most roads in the zones are over capacity. 1,547 residential parcels have a single way out. The hill zones hold 8,575 parcels and $8.7 billion of taxable value.

The next large earthquake is the biggest single danger

The U.S. Geological Survey gives a 33% chance of a magnitude 6.7 or larger earthquake on the Hayward or Rodgers Creek faults between 2014 and 2043. The City's hazard plan estimates that a large Hayward earthquake would kill about 100 people in Berkeley, displace 3,000 to 12,000 households and cut water to some homes for as long as six months. 293 soft-story apartment buildings have been retrofitted under the City's mandate; 1,047 other potentially vulnerable buildings face no requirement. On the shoreline, about 1,400 residents live in the tsunami zone, and the Bay has risen about eight inches since 1900.

The University and the Lab use far more energy than all of Berkeley's large businesses

UC Berkeley used about 790,000 MWh of energy in 2023, most of it gas burned in its own power plant, and Berkeley Lab about 176,000 MWh. All of Berkeley's large commercial and industrial buildings, the 218 that report under the City's energy ordinance, used about 162,000 MWh in 2024: the University and the Lab together used about 6 times as much. They draw about 1.6 million gallons of water a day, roughly 14% of the city's estimated use. Together they emitted 195,885 tonnes of CO₂e in 2024, equal to 45% of the City's community inventory, which leaves both out. The campus's gas plant is due to be replaced by an electric heating and cooling plant, its retirement now expected between 2030 and 2033. Under a 2021 settlement the University pays the City about $4.75 million a year, rising 3% annually; in return the City agreed not to sue over its growth plans or enrollment.

A $30 billion tax base, with the University outside it

Berkeley's 29,134 parcels carry $30.5 billion of net taxable value, about $376 million a year in base property tax for all agencies. 1,473 parcels pay none: the University, the City, schools, churches and other exempt owners.

The City's needs outrun its money

The City lists $2.1 billion of capital and maintenance needs, from buildings and storm drains to streets, parks and undergrounding, and funds $406.7 million of it over five years; the unfunded gap stays above $2.1 billion every year through 2031. Its General Fund has run a deficit every year since 2018, $31.3 million projected for FY2027, closed each time with one-time money, and the FY2027 budget cuts 164 positions. Pension payments doubled in nine years, to $77.9 million. The $300 million Measure U bond on the November ballot would cover about 14% of the stated need and none of the undergrounding.

How many people Berkeley may hold

Berkeley's population has been flat since 2020, at about 127,600, after a pandemic dip to 120,670 in 2021 when the campus emptied. Three plans point higher. The State requires Berkeley to plan for 8,934 new homes from 2023 to 2031. BerkeleyBuild's pipeline counts 11,222 homes approved or proposed but not yet built1. And student housing is growing. Berkeley has about 9,800 student beds in university and nonprofit dormitories: about 9,000 run by UC Berkeley (about 6,100 on the Southside, about 1,100 on the Northside, 1,000 at Clark Kerr and 772 at Anchor House downtown), plus Bowles Hall (188) and International House (about 600)2. Two UC buildings under construction add 2,738: Heumann House (1,113, fall 2027) and Bancroft-Fulton (1,625, fall 2028). A proposed building at Channing Way and Bowditch Street would add up to 2,000 more, for about 14,500 beds in all. If the pipeline were built at today's 2.13 people per home and the three dormitories were filled, Berkeley would gain about 23,900 residents in homes and about 4,700 in dormitories: about 156,000 people in all. That is a scenario, not a forecast; some approved projects will not be built, and many of UC's new beds will house students who now live in Berkeley apartments.

Research is turning into companies close to campus. Startups backed by Berkeley SkyDeck, the University's accelerator since 2012, have raised about $2.7 billion. Privacy as a rule

Berkeley sets public limits on watching its residents. Its 2018 Surveillance Technology Use and Community Safety Ordinance requires the City to weigh "the impacts … on civil rights and civil liberties" and to report every November on each technology it uses; the 2024 report covered body-worn cameras, GPS trackers, fixed cameras, license-plate readers, street-level imagery and drones. In March 2026 the Council adopted principles for the City's own use of artificial intelligence and asked for citywide guidelines.

Parks, the waterfront and the environment

Berkeley has more than fifty parks, though at about 2 acres per 1,000 residents it falls short of the State's base standard of 3. Its waterfront adds 100 acres of parks and open space and 7 miles of trails, including a 90-acre park on a former landfill (renamed in 2026 the Park at the Berkeley Waterfront) and Aquatic Park; the Berkeley Marina, with about 1,000 berths, is the largest public marina on the Bay. Tilden Regional Park, one of the East Bay Regional Park District's three original parks, adjoins the city in the hills. The environmental record is long: the nation's first curbside recycling began here in 1973, through the Ecology Center; the City set a zero-waste goal in 2005; and in 2019 it barred natural gas in new buildings, a law a federal appeals court struck down in 2023.

12 · Fire, flood and earthquake

The dangers, and what is being done

The three disasters Berkeley plans for: how likely each is, who and what is exposed, and the steps taken so far.

Earthquake

The Hayward Fault runs along the foot of the hills. The U.S. Geological Survey puts the chance of a magnitude 6.7 or larger earthquake on the Hayward or Rodgers Creek faults at 33% for 2014–2043, and anywhere in the Bay Area at 72%. For a large Hayward Fault earthquake (scenarios of magnitude 6.9 to 7.0), the City's hazard plan estimates about 100 deaths in Berkeley, 3,000 to 12,000 households displaced, over $2 billion in building damage, 620 buildings destroyed, 29,000 homes without power, and 1,800 breaks and 3,900 leaks in EBMUD's pipes, with water out for 6 weeks, some as long as 6 months. The land west of San Pablo Avenue is in the liquefaction zone, along with 101 miles (39%) of the City's sewers; 50 miles of sewer lie in the hills' earthquake-landslide zone.

How many cars leave the hills? The study counts vehicles, not parked cars: each household is assumed to drive out 1.14 vehicles, and workers, visitors and students drive out too. How many streets jam?

The study does not count them; it maps every congested road and names them. "One hour into the evacuation, the majority of the roads within the Evacuation Zones are operating at LOS F [demand over capacity] conditions including Grizzly Peak Blvd, Euclid Ave, Spruce St, Marin Ave, La Loma Ave, Gayley Rd, and Arlington Ave." Hill streets carry at most 900 cars an hour (flatland arterials about 1,700), and "severe congestion is expected to occur in all evacuation cases studied." The choke points are intersections, the Marin Avenue five-way roundabout among them, so red-curbing hill streets is "unlikely to substantially improve evacuation flow." Hills residents leaving early, combined with a faster departure, cut the time to clear 90% of vehicles by up to 68% (the last vehicles, by up to 14%).

Flood, tsunami and the rising Bay

The Bay has risen about eight inches since 1900. The City's plan finds that 5.5 feet of rise with a 25-year storm could flood Interstate 80 and as far east as Fifth Street, and that at 48 inches of rise, groundwater surfaces in West Berkeley north of University Avenue. Vision 2050 cited the State's range of 1.1 to 2.7 feet of rise by 2050. A tsunami would reach the shoreline first: about 1,400 residents in about 500 households live in the inundation zone, about 6,800 people are there by day, and about 450 businesses employ about 4,800; the Marina has 113 households living aboard. Inland, the storm drains were built for a 10-year storm; many parts over 90 years old; the 2022–23 storms brought more than 15 inches of rain in 20 days. The 100-year floodplain follows the shoreline, Aquatic Park, and Codornices and Strawberry creeks. The City's Stormwater Master Plan, due at the end of 2025, is still being prepared; no cost figure has been published.
