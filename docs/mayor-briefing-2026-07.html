More homes, healthy corridors:
what Berkeley's own records say works

An independent, receipt-level reconstruction of Berkeley's housing pipeline from primary sources — every building permit 2018–2026, the assessor's roll, the city's filings to the state — plus a fresh pull of the last 13 months, finished this week.

Every number on these slides traces to a public record. The method is published and audited: our count vs the city's official state filing differs by 130 units out of ~4,200 — and every row of that difference, in both directions, is named. → berkeleybuild.com/housing-audit.html

3 · Where time dies: delay, not denial

189 days median · 527 days 90th pct.

…from application to permit issuance for new-housing projects (928 permits). End to end, the slowest tenth of completed housing takes 3.5+ years from application to finished building. One 63-unit building (2527 San Pablo) waited 4.9 years for its permit.

  • Planning citywide: 743 approved vs 52 denied — the city rarely says no; it says wait.
  • The waiting room is real: 539 applications currently pending/incomplete.
JN-I baseline 2026-07-03; Day-2 harvest. Honesty rail: elapsed time includes applicant-side revisions — these are screens, not violation counts; publishing deemed-complete dates would settle it (see Asks).

5 · Housing did not hurt the corridors — vacancy is a separate problem

  • The corridors with the most new housing (Shattuck, University, Telegraph) sit at the healthiest end of the business-license table (19–21% of commercial parcels without an active license, vs 34% on San Pablo and 6% on Solano — a 5× spread unrelated to where housing was built).
  • The genuine weak spot: ground floors of new buildings. Preliminary: only 2 of the 14 largest corridor completions show an active non-rental business license at their street addresspreliminary — field check in progress
  • Policy lever: tenanting support and vacancy activation, not housing restriction. The city already taxes empty homes (Measure M); empty storefronts aren't even measured.
Day-3 overlay: Nov-2025 license roll × assessor commercial parcels (institutional parcels inflate some corridor shares; single-snapshot caveat applies — monthly license snapshots now running).

7 · Four asks that cost nothing and change everything

  • Publish the building-permit feed on the open-data portal the city already runs — it publishes business licenses today, but not a single permit dataset. (Our CPRA comparison found the internal report used for data requests captures 40–50% fewer filings than the public portal shows — undocumented filters.)
  • Publish deemed-complete dates — one field, and every statutory-clock question (the ADU 60-day rule, AB 2234) becomes answerable instead of arguable.
  • Publish the fee ledger — replace modeled fee figures with actuals.
  • Fix the three broken open datasets we hit this week: zoning (geometry, zero attributes), budget (expenses only, ends FY2016), and the permit-report filters above.
Each verified this week against the live portal and Accela; CPRA requests for items 2–3 are filed/in progress.

8 · This doesn't stop when the meeting ends

The record that produced these slides watches itself: the state filing is re-pulled and diffed on schedule (five mechanical error-detectors, anchor-validated), business licenses snapshot monthly, and the permit stream refreshes by harvest and CPRA. When the city's numbers move, we know the week it happens — and every claim keeps its receipt.
