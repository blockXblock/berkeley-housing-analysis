The short version

This is an independent reconstruction of Berkeley's housing record: every building permit from application to issuance to completion, built from the City's own primary records and the County assessor. It answers the questions California's Annual Progress Report (APR) asks, from the ground up, so the City's filed report can be checked line by line. Every number on this site comes from a public database you can query (Datasette) or rebuild yourself from the raw files (the curriculum).

Primary sources

  • The City's building-permit records, by public-records request. Berkeley's Annual Permit Report for 2018–2026, produced under the California Public Records Act (CPRA) in three spreadsheets on 2026-07-07. Each file is pinned by its SHA-256 checksum, so the build always reads exactly those bytes.
  • The City's permit portal (Accela). Per-permit detail pages, inspection histories, workflow dates and attachment lists, harvested from the public portal and saved as dated raw files.
  • Planning records. The City's planning log (also by CPRA) and Zoning Adjustments Board action minutes: which project applied, and when it was approved.
  • The Alameda County assessor. The County's open-data parcel layer (current as of February 2026): parcel numbers, geometry, and improvement values, plus the County's record of parcels that were split or merged.
  • The City's own maps. Address points and street-bounded block polygons, so every address resolves to a parcel and a block.
  • Documents. Staff reports, plan sets and affordability forms, archived with their checksums at docs.berkeleybuild.com so links survive reorganizations of the City's own storage.

The City's APR: the answer key, never an input

The City files its APR with the state Department of Housing and Community Development (HCD), which publishes every California city's filing on its open-data portal. We never build from that filing. We build from the sources above, then compare our result with the City's, row by row. Agreement is evidence; a difference is a finding, traced to its cause and written down. Using the City's filing as an input would make the comparison circular: we would only be checking the report against itself.

Differences cut both ways. The Housing Audit lists the City's reporting differences and our own mistakes in the same table, each with its evidence, and states the part of the gap no finding yet explains.

What we count, and how

  • Every permit, including ADUs. Accessory dwelling units and small projects are counted, not only large buildings; ADUs are a large share of what Berkeley completes.
  • A permit's housing effect is read, not guessed. Whether a permit adds, removes or only alters housing, and how many units, comes from reading each permit's description under HCD's definitions. The readings are stored with a checksum, so the same permits always give the same answer, and a re-reading is a new, dated evidence file.
  • Net new units. Units added minus units demolished, the measure HCD uses for production.
  • Completion. A unit is complete when the City finals its permit or issues a certificate of occupancy. The County's improvement value lags construction by a year or more, so a finaled permit outranks it.
  • Buildings and projects are assembled from events. Permits are grouped into buildings, and buildings into projects, from their records, parcels and addresses. A project is not a row someone typed in.
  • The RHNA cycle. A unit counts toward the 6th cycle when its building's first building permit was issued on or after 2022-06-30, the start of the projection period. Credit goes with the permit, not the completion.
  • University of California housing is in the pipeline total, counted in beds, and excluded from RHNA and the APR, because UC permits its own buildings.

What we record as unknown

Where the primary sources are silent (tenure, bedroom counts, some affordability levels), the record says unknown, with where we looked. It is never filled in from the City's APR or from a guess.

Corrections: every change is a row

When a source record is wrong or ambiguous, we do not edit the database by hand. The correction is a row in a public ledger (corrections/v4/ in the repository) with its evidence, and the next full rebuild applies it. The audit's findings are themselves a ledger: audit_findings.csv, one row per finding.

Checks that run on every rebuild

  • Nothing is lost on the way in. The three permit spreadsheets hold 40,243 rows, which become 107,374 permit events. Duplicates removed after that are each documented, so the live total (89,108) always equals the ingested total minus the documented removals. The check re-derives this from the files.
  • The site matches the data. Before any deploy, the published figures are compared with the database they came from; a mismatch stops the deploy.
  • Parcel numbers are joined carefully. County parcel numbers change when lots are split or merged, so a parcel number missing from the current assessor is flagged for review, never silently matched by nearest address.

How often it is updated

The database is rebuilt in full, from the raw files and the corrections ledger, whenever new primary data arrives: a new public-records production, a portal harvest, or a new County parcel layer. The site and the public Datasette are regenerated from that rebuild. Each page says when its data was last updated.

Limitations

  • Only what the City records. Work done without a permit is invisible.
  • Coverage starts in 2018, the first year of the permit reports produced to us. Earlier projects appear where later records refer to them.
  • Construction starts are rarely recorded as events; issuance and completion are.
  • Reading permits is a judgment. The readings follow HCD's definitions and are stored as evidence, but some descriptions are ambiguous; those are marked, and the audit reports the misreadings we have found.
  • No outcomes after occupancy: rents, tenure and residents are outside this record.

Rebuild it yourself, or for another city

The curriculum walks through the same build in free Colab notebooks: from the raw permit spreadsheets to a scored comparison with the City's APR. Another city's rebuild starts the same way: a public-records request for its annual permit report (or its open-data permit table), and an adjustment to the first notebook for that city's column names. HCD's portal carries every city's APR, so the answer key exists statewide.

Reporting an error

If you believe a number is wrong, open an issue on the project's GitHub repository with the number, the page or query it appears in, and the source you believe is right, with a link if you have one. A documented correction becomes a ledger row and appears on the site after the next rebuild.
