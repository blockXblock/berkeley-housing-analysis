# Who electrified Berkeley, and when the transformers went in — 2026-09-02

**Question asked:** are there articles or records of PG&E or its predecessors in
Berkeley, and when did the first transformers go in?

**Short answer:** yes, and the trail is good. I found and downloaded a primary source
that puts a named Berkeley substation in the trade press in **1910** — the same
substation PG&E photographed in its **2026** rate case. But the *first* transformer
cannot be dated from anything online; it needs the Bancroft city records or the
Berkeley Gazette. What follows is what is now established, and exactly where to look.

---

## 1. The corporate lineage

| Company | Dates | Berkeley relevance |
|---|---|---|
| **Oakland Gas Light Company** | founded 1866; renamed **Oakland Gas, Light and Heat Co.** c. 1884 | The long-established utility **serving Oakland and Berkeley** |
| **California Gas & Electric Corp. (CG&E)** | formed 1903 by de Sabla and Martin | Acquired Oakland Gas Light & Heat |
| **Pacific Gas and Electric** | **1905** — CG&E merged with San Francisco Gas & Electric | Berkeley's supply passes to PG&E |
| **Great Western Power Company** | founded Oakland 1905; **acquired by PG&E 1930** | A competing supplier into the East Bay; a Great Western branch terminated at Berkeley's Ridge Substation by 1910 |
| **Berkeley Lighting Company** | active c. 1910–11 | A **distinct local company** distributing at **4,000 volts** in Berkeley — see §3 |
| **Bay Counties Power Company** | Colgate→Oakland line **1901** | The 140-mile, 40,000 V (later 60,000 V) line, then the world's longest, crossing Carquinez Strait. Long-distance AC — which is to say, transformers |

## 2. The find: Ridge Substation, in print in 1910 and photographed in 2026

*Journal of Electricity, Power and Gas*, **Vol. 25 (Jul 1 – Dec 15, 1910)**,
"**The Ridge Substation at Berkeley**," by C. F. Adams. Verbatim opening:

> "To permit the removal of the Pacific Gas & Electric Company's high-tension wires
> from the cities of Oakland and Berkeley, the Ridge Substation was constructed. It is
> east and south of the abandoned quarry of the Spring Construction Company, and is now
> the terminus of the 60,000-volt lines from South Tower and Elmhurst, and also of one
> of the branches of the Great Western Power Company. The low-tension feeders (11,000
> volts) running from this station supply current to Berkeley and Oakland, and entirely
> replace the 60,000-volt lines which formerly passed through Berkeley and Oakland."

Further detail from the same article: built "entirely of concrete, steel, and glass…
designed to withstand any of the troubles and fire risks which are incident to
high-tension switching stations"; a fire wall divides the 60,000-volt oil-switch
section from the transformer and 11,000-volt section; oil switches with tops of
Catalina marble in concrete cells. A companion photograph is captioned "Air Switches
at the **North Berkeley Sub-Station**" — so Berkeley had at least two substations by 1910.

**Why this matters.** In the 2027 General Rate Case, Exhibit (PG&E-4) Figure 9-9 is
captioned "Three 4 kV transformers at **Ridge Substation, Berkeley**, installed in 1937."
**The same Berkeley substation is documented in 1910 and in 2026** — 116 years apart, in
two independent primary sources, one a trade journal and one a rate filing. Note also
that Ridge was built in 1910 *to get high-tension wire out of Berkeley's streets*: the
undergrounding argument the City is making to PG&E today is the same argument, in the
same city, restated.

**Berkeley was also fed from West Oakland.** *Journal of Electricity*, **Vol. 26
(Jan 7 – Jun 24, 1911)** describes the West Oakland substation's **13,200-volt** ring
bus, where "the two Oakland feeders and the two Berkeley feeders" could be tied
together or run as individual feeders "for the Berkeley substation."

## 3. The Berkeley Lighting Company, and a 4,000-volt thread worth pulling

*Journal of Electricity*, **Vol. 27 (Jul–Dec 1911)**, describing the University of
California's campus power plant, gives the most precise early Berkeley transformer
record I found:

> "Up to a year ago the distribution was 2300 volt 2 phase, obtained from the
> **Berkeley Lighting Co., 4000 V. power through two Scott connected 75 kw.
> transformers**…"

So around **1910**, a named Berkeley utility was delivering **4,000 volts**, stepped
down on the UC campus through a **Scott-connected pair of 75 kW transformers** (the
standard three-phase-to-two-phase connection of the era). The campus switchboard had a
dedicated panel for "Berkeley Lighting Company's incoming service."

**The hypothesis this raises.** PG&E's 2027 rate case says its 4 kV systems "have been
a part of PG&E's distribution network from at least the 1930s" and names Berkeley among
the oldest. Berkeley Lighting Company was distributing at **4,000 volts around 1910**.
Today's legacy network runs at **4,160 volts**. It is very plausible that **Berkeley's
4 kV system descends directly from the original local company's distribution voltage** —
i.e. that it is two decades older than PG&E's own "at least the 1930s."

**Stated as a hypothesis, not a finding.** 4,000 V was a common distribution voltage of
the period, so shared voltage is not by itself proof of a continuous network. Testing it
means tracing the Berkeley Lighting Co.'s franchise and its absorption into PG&E — a
Bancroft and Railroad Commission question.

## 4. Other Berkeley items in the trade press

- **Vol. 27 (1911)** — "BERKELEY, CAL.: The **Sierra and San Francisco Power Company**
  is planning for the installation of a power and lighting system at Berkeley. **No
  franchise is required** for this work and the City Council has been notified."
- **Vol. 37 (Jul–Dec 1916)** — Berkeley "has recently completed a street lighting system
  in the exclusive **Claremont** residence section," an installation "equipped with
  **special transformers** and new protective devices."
- **Vol. 56 (Jan–Jun 1926)** — Berkeley among cities using dome refractors, and among
  "San Francisco, Oakland, Berkeley and San Jose" that "will install future street
  lighting in accordance with carefully laid out plans."

## 5. So when *did* the first transformers go in?

**I cannot date the first one, and nothing online will.** Here is the honest bracket:

- **1888** — electric light and telephone in use in Berkeley (secondary sources).
- **1891** — Berkeley's first electric streetcar lines operating.
- **1894** — the earliest Sanborn fire insurance map of Berkeley (see §6); Sanborn
  sheets label "Electric Light Wks," "Power House" and "Sub Station," so this sheet
  should show whether Berkeley had a generating plant of its own and where.
- **1901** — Bay Counties' Colgate→Oakland line at 40,000 V. Long-distance AC transmission
  is impossible without step-up and step-down transformers, so transformers were
  certainly operating in the immediate area by then.
- **c. 1910** — the earliest transformers I can document *in Berkeley by name and rating*:
  the UC campus Scott-connected 75 kW pair, and the transformer section at Ridge Substation.

**The technical crux, for whoever chases this.** Transformers are only required for
**alternating current**. Berkeley's 1888-era lighting was very likely **arc lighting** on
series constant-current circuits, which needs no distribution transformer, and its 1891
streetcars ran on **DC**. The first true distribution transformers in Berkeley therefore
arrived with the first **AC incandescent distribution system** — almost certainly in the
1890s, and the date is a question about the Berkeley Lighting Company's (or Oakland Gas
Light & Heat's) first AC circuits in town. That is an archival question, not a web one.

## 6. Where to look next, ranked by cost

1. **Bancroft Library — City of Berkeley Records, `BANC MSS C-A 200`** (1878–1954, from
   the City Clerk's office). Includes **ordinances and petitions, 1881–1931**, described
   as covering electrical contracts and the creation of electrical infrastructure
   including street light placement. **This is where the first electric franchise
   ordinance is.** In person at Bancroft; copyright assigned to Bancroft, so publication
   needs written permission.
2. **Sanborn fire insurance maps of Berkeley**, free and digitised at the Library of
   Congress — **12 sheets, earliest 1894-05**, then 1911 (×2), 1917-11, 1929 (×3), 1950.
   Item IDs begin `loc.gov/item/sanborn00419_001` (1894). These are the cheapest
   possible way to *see* where Berkeley's early electrical plant stood.
3. **Berkeley Daily Gazette.** Free on **Google News Archive** (2 Oct 1911 – 31 Jul 1946).
   The **California Digital Newspaper Collection** (cdnc.ucr.edu) also carries it free but
   is behind a Cloudflare challenge that blocks scripted access — browse it by hand.
   **Newspapers.com** has 1894–1983 (354,430 pages) behind a paywall, and the pre-1911
   years are exactly the ones that matter.
4. **Berkeley Historical Society & Museum**, Berkeley History Center, Veterans Memorial
   Building, 1931 Center Street.
5. **Journal of Electricity, Power and Gas** — 1,017 issues on the Internet Archive;
   **34 bound volumes' full text now downloaded locally** (see below). Covers roughly
   1909–1926. Earlier volumes (1895–1908) exist as `sim_electrical-west_*` issues but
   are lending-restricted rather than plain-text downloads.
6. **Railroad Commission / CPUC legacy decisions**, `files.cpuc.ca.gov/LegacyCPUCDecisions
   AndResolutions/`. No directory browsing — reachable by direct file URL only, so find
   decision numbers by search first. The Railroad Commission took over utility regulation
   in 1912, so any Berkeley Lighting Co. acquisition or franchise transfer after that date
   leaves a decision.

## 7. Reproducing this

- `scripts/infrastructure/fetch_ia_journals.sh` — downloads the 36 bound volumes'
  OCR text from the Internet Archive.
- `scripts/infrastructure/search_archive_text.py <dir> <regex> [ctx] [max]` — searches
  them. **It flattens whitespace across the whole document before matching**, because
  OCR wraps lines arbitrarily: plain `grep` silently misses any phrase that straddles a
  line break, which is most of them. That single detail is why "Berkeley Lighting
  Company" was invisible to `grep` and visible to this.
- Text lives in `scratch/2026-09-02/journals/` (~130 MB, gitignored). One volume,
  `journalofelectri00sanfrich`, returned no text and one other failed — 34 of 36 usable.
