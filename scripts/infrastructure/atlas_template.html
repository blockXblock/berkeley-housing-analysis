Finding 01

About 4,500 Berkeley utility poles are already public — nobody had looked

The project began from the premise that city streetlights are downloadable and utility poles are not. The first half is right. The second turns out to be only half right.

Berkeley's streetlight layer carries a field called POLEMAT — pole material. The City does not erect wooden poles to hold up streetlights; it uses metal standards. So when a city streetlight record says its pole is wood, it is almost always a light hung on somebody else's pole — a joint-use PG&E/AT&T distribution pole.

What comes attached to them

Of the 4,499 wood-pole records, 4,456 carry a UTIL_PN — a utility pole number — giving 4,428 distinct pole IDs, overwhelmingly in PG&E's B#### Berkeley series, at 4,487 distinct coordinates. Each also carries a PG&E SAID and SPID (service agreement and service point), a nearest address, and a mounting height.

The layer separately records FIELD_PN, the tag physically read off the pole during the 2014 Tanko Lighting LED conversion audit. It agrees with the utility's own number on 95.6% of wood poles; the disagreements are mostly prefix noise (BMI451 against MI451).

Independent check

Wood poles avoid the undergrounded blocks — exactly as they should

If POLEMAT=Wood really is a proxy for “an overhead utility pole stands here,” wood poles should be scarce inside Berkeley's Underground Utility Districts, where the wires are buried. Testing all 7,969 lights against the 3,449 UUD parcels:

Streetlight polewithin 30 m of a UUD parcel
Wood (utility poles)545 / 4,499  =  12.1%
Metal (city standards)2,261 / 3,304  =  68.4%

Wood poles are 5.6× less likely to sit in an undergrounded district. Two independent City datasets, never built to be compared, agree. That is the strongest confirmation available without asking PG&E.

Limits of this number — read before quoting it
  • It is a floor, not a count. This method only finds utility poles that happen to carry a city streetlight. Poles mid-block, on unlit blocks, and in rear lots are invisible to it. The real total is higher — by an unknown amount.
  • Wood is strong evidence, not proof of joint-use ownership. Confirming it means spot-checking a sample against street imagery and asking PG&E to verify the B#### series.
  • Two records of 7,969 have null geometry in the shapefile, but both carry latitude and longitude attributes, so all 7,969 are placeable. Nothing was dropped.
Finding 02 · the load-bearing gap

There is no transformer layer. Anywhere.

All 504 layers on Berkeley's GIS server were enumerated and counted. Not one of them is a transformer. The city that hosts the poles does not hold a record of what hangs on them.

Why the manufacture year is the field that decides everything

Under 40 CFR 761.2, any mineral-oil-filled electrical equipment manufactured before 2 July 1979 whose PCB concentration has not been established must be assumed to be PCB-contaminated — at or above 50 ppm. All pole-top and pad-mounted distribution transformers made before that date must also be assumed mineral-oil filled.

This is the spine of the security story, and it is a regulation rather than an allegation. Every pre-July-1979 pole-mounted transformer still hanging over a Berkeley street is, by federal rule, presumed PCB-contaminated unless PG&E has tested it. Two fields — manufacture year and test status — convert “thousands of ageing transformers” into a defensible number. They are the first ask in the data request.

What we are deliberately not doing

We are not publishing an estimated Berkeley transformer count. A customers-per-transformer ratio would be a fabricated number wearing the costume of a finding. There are two honest routes to a real one: ask PG&E (the drafted request), or count them from imagery — pole-mounted transformers are visually unmistakable, and the ~4,400 pole coordinates above are a ready-made sample frame for a stratified survey with a stated confidence interval. That fallback is publishable precisely because its method is stated.

Finding 03 · from PG&E's own filings

67,000 overloaded transformers, replaced at about 450 a year

The inventory is not public. But 2,887 pages of PG&E's rate case and wildfire plan are, and they say more than expected — including Berkeley by name, twice.

PG&E is currently tracking over 67,000 overloaded service transformers in the system, but because the CBR is low, PG&E is limiting transformer replacements to between 400 and 488 transformers per year.
Exhibit (PG&E-4), 2027 General Rate Case, p. 9-35
137–168years to clear that backlog at PG&E’s own stated replacement rate
67,000overloaded service transformers PG&E says it is already tracking
~450replaced per year, because the cost-benefit ratio is low
1937install year of three 4 kV transformers at Ridge Substation, Berkeley

CBR is cost-benefit ratio. The arithmetic is not a gotcha — the sentence exists to justify a funding request — but it is the finding the project was looking for: the utility knows where 67,000 overloaded transformers are, and has decided that replacing about 450 a year is what the economics support. The same testimony forecasts a further 5,000 to 9,400 newly overloaded transformers from EV charging alone between 2027 and 2030.

Berkeley is named twice

Berkeley is one of PG&E's legacy 4 kV systems — networks dating "from at least the 1930s," found "especially in California's oldest downtown areas, such as San Francisco, Oakland, Berkeley, San Jose, Stockton, Santa Cruz, and Monterey." The East Bay 4 kV group runs from Richmond to Hayward, and over 350,000 customers system-wide are still served at 4 kV.

Then PG&E published a photograph of Berkeley's oldest transformers to make its own case. Figure 9-9 of the Electric Distribution testimony is captioned "Three 4 kV transformers at Ridge Substation, Berkeley, installed in 1937." Two pages earlier, the filing cites the U.S. Department of the Interior for the proposition that a transformer's average expected life is about 40 years.

Eighty-nine years old, against a forty-year design life — and it is the utility making the point, not us. The 4 kV conversion programme starts in 2027 at $29M, rising to about $170M a year through 2030, on a stated 15-to-20-year timeframe to convert all of it.

The maintenance posture, verbatim
Distribution overhead transformers are partly proactively managed and generally run to condition.
2023–2025 Wildfire Mitigation Plan, p. 512

Run to condition means replaced on failure, or on an inspection finding. Not on a schedule. The filing names the conditions watched for: corrosion, physical damage, and leaking oil.

A correction this project has to absorb: the fire premise is the weak one

We expected aging transformers to be a leading wildfire risk. PG&E's own wildfire risk model does not support that. From the Wildfire Distribution Risk Model v3 target dataset:

Transformers sit among the lowest ignition rates in the model, well under the 3.69% average, and leaking units produced zero ignitions in 1,126 events. Leading with wildfire would be contradicted by the utility's own filing. What survives is stronger and cleaner: 1,126 recorded leaks is an oil-on-the-ground number that stands whether or not anything burns; 67,000 against 450 a year is a scandal on its own arithmetic; and 1937 equipment is simply old.

What this unlocks

We refused to estimate a Berkeley transformer count because any customers-per-transformer ratio would have been invented. It no longer has to be: PG&E's rebuttal testimony states that the average number of customers per residential transformer is 7. With a Census housing-unit denominator that becomes a derivation from two cited numbers. Two cautions travel with it, and must be printed alongside any figure: the ratio is residential only, and it does not separate overhead from underground — in a city that has already buried 49% of its arterials, it would overstate the pole-mounted count.

One layer is not what its host implies

The PG&E gas pipeline layer is served from Berkeley's public-safety map, but it is regional: it runs east to longitude −121.56 and south to latitude 37.46, out past the Central Valley. Only 322 of its 5,411 features touch Berkeley. Anyone quoting 5,411 as a Berkeley figure would be wrong by a factor of seventeen. The map above is clipped to the in-city features; the holdings table reports both numbers. It also carries only transmission and 60-psig-and-above distribution — the low-pressure street mains that serve individual houses are not public at all.

Also enumerated but not yet pulled: storm inlets (4,791), storm structures (1,183), storm discharge points (1,040), creek culverts (378), lift stations (7).

The pipes are old, and the records say so

EBMUD water mains · 13,396 segments

63% were laid before 1960. The single biggest decade is the 1930s (2,621 segments, 20%). The oldest segment on record dates to the 1860s. Material type is 100% null in the public layer — an honest gap, and a good first question for EBMUD.

PG&E gas · 322 segments in Berkeley

48% were installed before 1980; the largest decades are the 1980s and 1960s. 46 segments sit in a High Consequence Area. About 38 miles of line. Two warnings: the layer holds only 60 psig and above — the low-pressure mains that actually feed houses are not in it — and its records were last modified between 2007 and 2016, so it is a snapshot, not today's network.

The contrast worth building the story on

Berkeley's sanitary sewer has a funded, scheduled, court-supervised rehabilitation programme — the layer carries rehab years, rehab specs, a twelve-year plan and a planning cost for each segment, and 4,939 of 7,744 mains are already HDPE, meaning they have been relined. The pipes are being fixed on a clock. The transformers overhead have no such clock, no such register, and no public count at all. That asymmetry is the story.

What happens now

Drafted and waiting for review

  • PG&E data request — built around the 4,428 pole IDs we already hold, so the ask is “attach transformer attributes to these poles,” not “send us your pole database.” Asks for count per pole, kVA, manufacture year, fluid type and PCB test status.
  • CPUC Public Records Act request — scoped only to records the Commission already holds: GO 165 reports, WMP data tables, the pole-and-conduit proceeding record, GRC asset exhibits.
  • Both now sharper. Ask PG&E for the Berkeley subset of its own 67,000 — breaking out a published number by city is a far smaller ask than an asset register — plus the Ridge Substation record and Berkeley's place in the 4 kV conversion schedule.

Cheaper things to try first

  • Mine the 2027 GRC exhibits and the WMP — done 2026-09-02, see Finding 03. Still missing from them: a system-wide unit count, and the age curve, which is an image (Figure 9-8, p. 9-55) that needs reading by eye.
  • Check whether GRIP registration is genuinely open, or restricted to interconnection applicants.
  • Pull the remaining storm layers, and add the Accela asset service, which mirrors public works assets under a different schema.
  • Spot-check a sample of wood poles against street imagery — validating the pole finding and producing the first real transformers-per-pole rate at the same time.
