Measure U, examined

Berkeley asks voters on November 3, 2026 to authorize a $300 million general-obligation bond for infrastructure. This site explains, in plain language, how Berkeley property taxes actually work; reconciles the City's official numbers with the county assessor roll, parcel by parcel; tests the campaign's claims; measures the measure against the City's own Vision 2050 framework; and states the conditions under which the Vision 2050 professionals support it. Every figure is derived from primary sources — the resolution, the Tax Rate Statement, 37 real bills, 111/111 line-item reconciliations, and the 27,661-parcel assessor roll — by a re-runnable, gate-checked pipeline.

Start here: the three ways Berkeley taxes its households

Before judging a new tax, know the ones you already pay. Berkeley households pay through three different channels, and they work in completely different — often opposite — ways.

1 · Tax on your home's VALUE

About 1.23% of your home's assessed value every year (all agencies combined). Here's the catch: under Proposition 13, assessed value is not what your home is worth — it's what you paid for it, plus at most 2% per year. So Berkeley splits into two groups. If you bought decades ago, your taxable value crawls upward and is now far below market. If you bought recently — or built something new — your taxable value is your full purchase price or construction cost. Identical houses on the same block can carry taxable values 15× apart. When a long-held house sells, its taxable value jumps to the sale price overnight.

Measure U adds to this channel.
2 · Taxes on your home's SIZE

Berkeley's special taxes — schools, library, parks, fire, streets — mostly ignore value entirely. They charge by the square foot of your building: about $1.94 per sq ft per year, plus about $193 in flat per-parcel charges. A 1,900 sq ft house pays the same whether it was bought in 1975 or last Tuesday. This channel runs opposite to channel 1: it's heaviest, as a share of what the county says your house is worth for taxes, on long-held, low-assessed homes.

On a real Berkeley bill, this channel is the majority of the total.
3 · Tax on what you BUY

Sales tax — currently 10.25% in Berkeley, rising to 10.75% if companion Measure V passes. It taxes spending, not property, so renters pay it directly. Online purchases delivered to Berkeley are generally charged the same rate, but the locally-kept share of an online sale is often pooled countywide rather than credited to Berkeley the way an in-store sale is — one reason cities keep returning to property measures.

Measure V (same ballot) raises this channel.

Read that first row again. The longest-held homes pay the least in dollars — but 77% of their bill comes from size-based charges they can't reduce, and their bill is the highest share of assessed value (5.5% vs 1.5%). Any honest debate about a new tax has to say which channel it uses and who that lands on. Measure U uses channel 1.

The hidden assumption: who grows the base

Reproducing the advertised $22.14 average requires the base to grow 4.2–4.7% per year (its 40-year average base: 2.25× today's). Prop 13 caps parcels that don't change hands at 2%. The remainder — the orange share — must come from reassessment at sale and new construction (plus bounded, cyclical Prop-8 restorations).

Growth from sitting owners at the 2% cap Newcomer wedge — sales & new construction: 53–58% of all assumed growth
The point: the advertised low average rate is financed by future buyers and future buildings. If the base grew only at the Prop-13 cap, the identical bond would average $33 per $100,000 — 1.5× the advertised figure. The buildings completed since 2018 already visible in the roll (33 projects, 2,237 units, $1.1B, an undercount) are that wedge arriving.

Who pays

Three terms, in plain English, then the numbers.

“Ad-valorem” is Latin for on the value: your Measure U bill is one citywide rate multiplied by your parcel's assessed value. Big assessed value, big bill; small, small.

“Assessed value is acquisition-based” means the county doesn't tax what your property is worth — it taxes what you (or your building's developer) paid, plus at most 2% a year since. That's Proposition 13. So this bond automatically bills recent buyers and new buildings hardest, and bills the longest-held properties least — regardless of anyone's wealth or ability to pay.

The shares below don't depend on which rate applies (they are “rate-invariant”): whatever rate the City ends up levying — the $22.14 average, the $35 peak — everyone's bill scales by the same factor, so each group's slice of the total never changes. The slices are the honest thing to argue about.

Who ARE the “top 1%” of parcels? (Not homeowners.)

The top 276 parcels — the ones paying a fifth of the whole bond — are almost entirely large buildings, not houses: 53% of the tier's value is apartment buildings (133 parcels — including the big projects completed in the last decade), 31% is commercial and industrial (West Berkeley plants, downtown offices and hotels), and 13% is institutional. Exactly 6 single-family homes make the top 1%. Widen the lens and homes take over: you enter the top 25% at $1,260,991 of assessed value — a recently-bought Berkeley house — and single-family homes are that tier's largest group (4,253 of 6,915 parcels, 41% of its value).

So the plain-language version: at the very top, Measure U is a tax on big buildings — mostly apartments, which pass it into rents. Below that, it is a tax on whoever bought a home most recently. The half of Berkeley that has held longest pays 15% of the bond.

The campaign's claims, against the record

From the ballot label, the staff reports, and the City's public materials. None of these claims is false; each is examined for what it leaves out.

The claimWhat the record shows
“$22.14 per $100,000 assessed value” (ballot label) True as a 40-year average on a base assumed to reach 2.2× today's. The disclosed peak is $35; the staff-reported average combined with existing GO debt is $44.13 (a figure the county rate history suggests needs reconciling — existing city GO alone has averaged $51.90 per $100k over 12 years); the same debt on today's base is $67. Several numbers, one levy — voters see the smallest.
“Subject to independent oversight and audits” (ballot label) The text provides two existing council-appointed commissions, an annual City Manager report, and a City Auditor audit at least every three years. No dedicated citizens' bond oversight committee — unless the Council creates one (see Oversight).
“Fire stations, emergency response, parks… climate resiliency” (ballot label) The named categories are real, but the project list is explicitly non-exhaustive and non-binding: any acquisition or improvement of real property qualifies, including projects never shown to the public, and proceeds may reimburse money already spent.
“Documented in… the City's Vision 2050 Framework” (impartial analysis) Vision 2050 is cited by name while its central sequence — plan, prioritize, then ask — is inverted: project-by-project prioritization is deferred until after voters approve the money (see Vision 2050).
“Over $1.5–2.1 billion in unfunded needs” (findings; webpage) The City's own stated need moved from $882M (2020) to >$1B (Dec 2025) to >$1.5B (adopted CIP) to >$2.1B (proposed CIP) — a 2.4× spread that is itself evidence no stable program plan sits under the ask.
“$313M program capacity” (staff report) Includes $40.5M (13%) for staffing and implementation, and the measure permits reimbursing prior expenditures — meaningful General-Fund relief for a city projecting $31M/$29M deficits, which is not what the label advertises.
“Berkeley's GO tax ($270) is far below Oakland's ($660) and Albany's ($685)” (work session) Arithmetically correct — county data confirms the ratios. But it counts only the GO-bond line. Berkeley financed through parcel taxes instead: on the reconciled real bill, city GO debt is $357 while city parcel taxes are $7,045. The comparison measures the one channel Berkeley uses least; on total ad-valorem rates Berkeley sits mid-pack among Alameda County's 14 cities.
“Delay makes it more expensive” (City Manager) True — construction escalation is real, and it applies equally to the ~$1.7B of need this bond does not fund. Urgency argues for a complete financing plan at least as strongly as for this $300M.

The lifecycle sort the City never published

Every capital dollar creates a different operating future. Sorting the City's own 36-project list (a v0 professional sort — the City has published no per-project O&M or design life anywhere):

Durable, long-life, low carry — ~$42M (15%): seawall, marina piles, Bay Trail, sidewalks, ADA ramps, pathsCatch-up renewal of existing assets — ~$150M (55%): pools, restrooms, play structures, elevators, end-of-life HVAC, 911 center, seismic partials, Fire Stations 4 & 6New/expanded capacity → permanent new O&M — ~$82M (30%): Frances Albrier replacement, new Adeline parkland, behavioral-health building, Fire Training Center
Final collection is FY 2066/67 — while artificial turf (~10-yr life), play structures (~15–20), HVAC (~20–25) and elevators (~25) will be worn out and replaced, possibly twice, on the debt that bought them. That mismatch is precisely what Vision 2050's cradle-to-grave strategy exists to prevent — and what the oversight conditions below are designed to fix.

Oversight: what the measure provides, and what support requires

The measure's own §6(F) assigns oversight to two commissions “or their successors” — so every body below can be created by ordinary Council ordinance or resolution. No charter amendment, no second election. The Vision 2050 professionals' support is conditioned on one omnibus Implementation & Accountability Resolution, adopted before ballots print.

The position, stated plainly. The people who wrote Vision 2050 spent years demanding exactly this discipline. Their support for Measure U is the exchange: signatures and credibility for a September resolution adopting all seven commitments, with dates. If the resolution fails, the support — and its central claim — fails with it.
